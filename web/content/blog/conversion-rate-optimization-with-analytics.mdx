Conversion rate optimization gets talked about like it's a discipline that requires a dedicated team, a series of A/B tests, and months of data collection. In reality, most conversion problems are obvious once you know where to look — and fixing them requires tools you probably already have.

Here's the process that actually works.

## Start With the Funnel, Not the Homepage

The most common mistake in CRO is starting at the wrong place. Teams obsess over their homepage bounce rate while ignoring the checkout flow where most of the money is actually lost.

Before you do anything else, map out your most important conversion path. For most businesses this is one of:

- **Acquisition funnel**: landing page → pricing page → signup → onboarding → first meaningful action
- **Purchase funnel**: product page → cart → checkout → confirmation
- **Lead funnel**: blog/ad → landing page → form → thank you

Now look at conversion rates at each step. The step with the biggest drop-off is where you start. Not the homepage. Not the most-visited page. The step that loses the most potential customers.

## Quantify the Problem Before You Diagnose It

Once you've identified the leaky step, you need to know what you're working with before you start watching replays.

Pull the numbers:
- How many sessions enter this step per month?
- What percentage complete it?
- What's the absolute number of drop-offs?

If 2,000 people per month reach your pricing page and 1,740 leave without clicking anything, that's 87% drop-off. If your conversion to paid is 3%, getting that step from 13% to 20% would increase your total conversions by 54% without changing any other part of the funnel. That's your leverage point.

Knowing the magnitude of the problem tells you how much it's worth fixing and keeps you from spending a week optimizing a step that accounts for 3% of your drop-off.

## Use Heatmaps to Form a Hypothesis

Before you watch session recordings (which takes time), use the heatmap to form a hypothesis about what might be going wrong.

For the leaky step:

**Click map first.** Is your primary CTA being clicked? Is something else drawing more attention? Are users clicking on non-interactive elements (suggesting confusion about what's clickable)?

**Scroll map second.** Are users reaching your CTA? If 60% of sessions never scroll to where your "Get Started" button is, the problem isn't the button — it's that users aren't reaching it.

**On mobile specifically.** If more than 40% of your traffic is mobile (check your device breakdown), look at the mobile heatmap separately. Mobile behavior is often dramatically different.

The heatmap should give you a working hypothesis: "Users aren't scrolling to the CTA" or "Users are clicking on the feature descriptions but not on the pricing buttons" or "The form seems to be getting attention but nobody submits it."

## Validate With Session Replays

Now watch recordings — specifically filtered to users who hit this step and did NOT convert.

You're looking for:

**Confusion signals:** Moving the cursor back and forth, hovering over an element repeatedly without clicking, scrolling up and down multiple times. This suggests the user wants to do something but can't figure out how.

**Rage clicks:** Clicking the same element multiple times quickly. Usually means a broken interaction — a button that appears clickable but doesn't respond, a form that fails silently, a link that goes somewhere unexpected.

**Premature exits:** User lands on the page, barely scrolls, and leaves within 5–10 seconds. This is a relevance mismatch — the page didn't match what the user expected based on how they arrived.

**Form abandonment patterns:** For forms specifically, look at where in the form users stop. If most abandonment happens at the "credit card" field, price is the barrier. If it happens at a mandatory phone number field, the ask is too much. If it's the email field, you may have a trust problem.

Watch 10–15 recordings. You're looking for patterns, not edge cases. If you see the same confusion or friction point in 4 out of 10 recordings, that's a real problem worth fixing.

## The Five Most Common Conversion Problems

After going through this process on hundreds of pages, the same issues come up repeatedly:

**1. The CTA is below the fold and users aren't scrolling.**
Fix: Move the primary CTA higher. Add a sticky header with the CTA. Shorten the page above the button.

**2. The value proposition isn't clear.**
Users aren't clicking because they don't understand what they're signing up for, buying, or getting. The headline and subheading need to answer "what do I get and why does it matter to me" in about 3 seconds.

Fix: Rewrite the headline to be outcome-focused ("Reduce checkout abandonment by 30%") rather than feature-focused ("Advanced analytics for your website").

**3. The form asks for too much too early.**
Every additional field in a form reduces completion rate. Asking for a phone number, company size, or credit card before you've delivered value will kill your conversion.

Fix: Reduce to the minimum viable fields. Move optional fields to a second step after the user has committed to starting.

**4. There's a technical error on mobile.**
Buttons that don't work, forms that don't submit, pages that overflow their container. These are invisible if you only test on desktop.

Fix: Filter session replays to mobile specifically. Watch 5 recordings. You'll find it.

**5. Users leave to find social proof.**
Users arrive at a pricing or signup page, spend time on it, then leave to search for reviews or comparisons. They never come back because the friction of returning is too high.

Fix: Add testimonials, review counts, and social proof directly on the conversion page. Don't make users leave to find what should be right in front of them.

## Measure, Ship, Measure Again

CRO only works if you measure before and after. Pick a single change, implement it, and run it for at least two weeks with enough traffic to see a meaningful signal.

Don't change five things at once. You won't know what worked.

Don't declare victory after three days. Small sample sizes are misleading. Wait for statistical significance or at least enough sessions to see a clear trend.

And don't stop after one iteration. Conversion optimization is a continuous process. Once you fix the biggest leak in your funnel, there's always a second-biggest leak.
