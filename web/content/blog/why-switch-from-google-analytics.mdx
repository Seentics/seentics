Google Analytics is the most widely deployed piece of software on the web. It's on an estimated 56% of all websites. And yet, over the past two years, a meaningful number of teams have quietly stopped using it.

This isn't a niche movement. It's showing up in product team Slack channels, in engineering discussions about compliance, and in the decisions of companies that got burned by a regulatory audit. Here's what's driving it.

## The GA4 Migration Was a Disaster

In July 2023, Google shut down Universal Analytics and forced everyone to GA4. If you weren't prepared — and most teams weren't — you lost your historical data comparison, your saved reports, your custom dashboards, and your muscle memory for a tool you'd been using for years.

GA4 is a genuinely different product. It's event-based instead of session-based, which is more flexible in theory but significantly harder to set up correctly in practice. The interface was redesigned from scratch. Reports that took two clicks in UA now require building custom explorations. Standard metrics like "bounce rate" changed their definition.

The result: teams that had been perfectly happy with their analytics setup suddenly had to re-learn everything, rebuild their reporting, and accept a period of broken data during the transition — all without choosing to change anything.

That disruption opened a window. Teams that were already evaluating alternatives had a strong reason to make the switch. Teams that had never questioned GA before started asking: if we have to rebuild anyway, why rebuild on Google's terms?

## The Compliance Risk Is Real

In January 2022, Austria's data protection authority ruled that using Google Analytics violates GDPR because it transfers European visitor data to US servers. France, Italy, Denmark, and other EU member states followed with similar rulings.

The core issue is that US surveillance law (FISA Section 702) allows US intelligence agencies to access data held by US companies. The GDPR requires that personal data transferred outside the EU be protected to an equivalent standard. The regulators concluded that Google Analytics data transfers do not meet that standard.

Google has made technical attempts to address this (IP anonymization, data processing agreements, server-side proxying), but regulators have generally found these insufficient. The legal risk for EU-based businesses — or businesses with significant EU traffic — is ongoing.

For many teams, the calculus is simple: the compliance risk of staying on GA isn't worth it when alternatives exist.

## The Data Doesn't Answer the Questions That Matter

This is the less-discussed problem with GA, and arguably the bigger one for product teams.

Google Analytics tells you how many people visited a page, where they came from, and how long they stayed. What it doesn't tell you:

- Why did 74% of users leave the pricing page without converting?
- Which part of the checkout flow is causing abandonment on mobile?
- Are users actually reading the product description or skipping straight to the CTA?
- What did the users who converted do differently from the ones who didn't?

To answer those questions in the GA ecosystem, you need to layer on Hotjar, FullStory, or similar tools. More accounts, more scripts on the page, more monthly costs, and data that lives in separate systems that don't talk to each other.

Teams that have lived with this fragmentation long enough eventually ask: why are we paying for five tools that should be one?

## The Consent Banner Problem

Standard Google Analytics (without additional configuration) requires a cookie consent banner in most jurisdictions. The banner degrades user experience. It also degrades your data — users who decline tracking are invisible in your analytics, creating a systematic bias toward users who are more permissive about tracking.

In practice, a significant percentage of visitors opt out. On some European sites, opt-out rates above 30–40% are common. That means your "traffic" data is missing a large segment of your actual visitors.

Cookieless analytics tools — those that don't use persistent identifiers and don't transfer data across sites — can often operate without consent banners under ePrivacy and GDPR. Your data is more complete, your pages are less cluttered, and your legal exposure is lower.

## What to Look For in a Replacement

If you're evaluating alternatives, here's what actually matters:

**Privacy model.** Does the tool use cookies or persistent identifiers? Where is data stored? Does it require a consent banner? If you have EU visitors, these aren't optional questions.

**Behavioral depth.** Traffic metrics are a commodity. The differentiator is session replays, heatmaps, and funnel analysis — the tools that answer *why* instead of just *what*.

**Self-hosting option.** For teams with strict data residency requirements, the ability to run the analytics stack on your own infrastructure is often non-negotiable. Even teams without that requirement often prefer it for the control it provides.

**Simplicity.** GA4 proved that complexity is a cost. A tool your team actually looks at and acts on is worth more than a more powerful tool that nobody uses correctly.

**Real-time data.** 24-hour processing delays were acceptable in 2012. In 2026, if you shipped a change at 2pm and you can't see its impact until tomorrow, you've lost the ability to respond quickly.

## The Switch Is Simpler Than It Looks

The biggest obstacle to migrating off Google Analytics is usually inertia, not technical complexity. The actual implementation — removing GA, installing a replacement — takes less than an hour. The harder part is convincing stakeholders who have years of GA familiarity.

The case is straightforward: your current tool is creating compliance risk, missing a significant portion of your visitors, and not answering the questions that drive product decisions. The replacement is simpler, faster, and tells you more about what users actually do.

Historical data is the main loss. GA data stays in GA; you can't migrate it. But for most teams, the analytics from more than 12 months ago isn't actually influencing daily decisions anyway.
